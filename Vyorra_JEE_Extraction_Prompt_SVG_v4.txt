── Which field a figure belongs in ──────────────────────────────────────

This is the one distinction I'd most like to get right, so a quick guide:

  solution_svg  — a figure that appears next to the worked solution: a curve sketched
                  while solving, a constructed triangle, a circle drawn tangent to
                  something, a free-body diagram. In a solutions booklet like this one,
                  this is where MOST figures belong. If a diagram sits beside the
                  algebra rather than beside the question statement, it goes here.

  question_svg  — a figure that is part of the original question statement, i.e. the
                  student would have seen it in the exam. Typically the question text
                  says "as shown in the figure" or the question is unanswerable without
                  the diagram.

  option_svgs   — 4-element array, index 0=A … 3=D, for "pick the correct graph /
                  circuit / structure" questions, one drawing per slot.

When you genuinely can't tell which side a figure belongs to, put it in solution_svg —
I'd rather have it in the slightly wrong field than not have it. Please don't leave a
printed figure out of the JSON entirely just because its role is ambiguous.

(For context: my previous version of these notes said solution figures were rare and
usually absent. That was written for clean question papers and is wrong for this PDF —
please ignore that idea if you saw it. Here, a figure beside the solution working is
the normal case.)


── Math formatting — one small but important detail ────────────────────────

Math needs plain single dollar signs with no backticks:

  correct:    "then $b$ is equal to $-128$"
  incorrect:  "then $`b`$ is equal to $`-128`$"

The backtick form ($`b`$) is what I keep receiving, and my LaTeX renderer treats the
backtick as a literal character, so it shows up on screen inside the equation. Plain
$ … $ only, please. Ordinary prose stays unformatted.


── Output shape ────────────────────────────────────────────────────

A top-level JSON array (starting with [ and ending with ]), in a code block in your
reply. Not wrapped as {"questions": [...]}, since my parser expects the bare array.

Keys per question, in this order:
  question_number, subject, unit, chapter, topic, year, month, day, shift,
  question_text, question_svg, tables, option_a, option_b, option_c, option_d,
  option_svgs, optionTables, correct_answer, solution, solution_svg,
  has_image, image_description, question_type

Notes on the less obvious ones:
  optionTables    — exact camelCase. 4-element array, index 0=A, each entry either
                    {"headers":[...],"rows":[[...]]} or null.
  tables          — [{"position":"after_question_text","headers":[...],"rows":[[...]]}]
                    or null. Please don't repeat table data inside question_text.
                    A table cell that is itself a figure: {"text": "", "svg": "<svg …/>"}
  correct_answer  — "A"–"D" for MCQ, joined letters like "AC" for MSQ, a number for
                    INTEGER, or the string "NONE" for none-of-these.
  question_type   — "MCQ", "MSQ" or "INTEGER".
  has_image       — true if you supplied any SVG anywhere in that question, including a
                    solution_svg. (I've been getting has_image:false on questions that
                    do have a solution figure, which hides them from my review screen.)
  image_description — null. (The SVG replaces it; my UI has nowhere to show text here.)

One quirk of my UI: when an option is purely a figure with no text of its own, its text
field needs to be a zero-width space "\u200b" rather than "" or null, otherwise the four
option slots collapse in the layout.

On the solutions themselves: a clean final derivation is what I need. If your working
disagrees with the printed answer, please just give the correct derivation and add a
short note at the end like "(printed answer key says C)" — I've been getting solutions
that contain a wrong derivation followed by "...recompute: actual answer is X", and I
can't publish those to students.

── What my importer needs from the SVG ────────────────────────────────────

These are constraints of my renderer, not style preferences:

• Root element <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" viewBox="0 0 W H"> … </svg>.
  I need both the xmlns and the viewBox — the viewBox is what lets it scale on phones.
  Please write the namespace as a bare URL, not as a markdown link.
• One line, valid inside a JSON string: internal quotes escaped as \", no raw newlines.
• Presentation attributes rather than <style> or CSS classes:
  stroke="#111" stroke-width="2" fill="none". Dark strokes on white/transparent.
• Nothing external and no scripting: my sanitizer strips <script>, <foreignObject>,
  <image>, <iframe>, on* handlers and external URLs before rendering, so anything in
  those would silently vanish and leave a broken figure.
• Fonts limited to font-family="serif" or "sans-serif", font-size 11–16. Unicode inside
  <text> is fine (Ω, θ, μ, °, →, ², −).
• Keep each figure under about 12 KB — primitives only (line, path, circle, rect,
  polyline, polygon, ellipse, text, g, marker, defs).


── What makes a figure usable, by type ────────────────────────────────────

The general rule: a student should be able to follow the question or the solution from
your drawing alone. Every quantity, angle, label and connection that the working depends
on should be visible, and nothing should contradict the PDF. If a label is unreadable in
the scan, please leave it out rather than guessing at a value.

Coordinate geometry / conics (most of this PDF) — draw the axes when they matter, label
the curve or give its equation as <text>, mark and label the named points ((1,2), C(h,k)),
show tangent and normal lines as straight lines touching at the right place, and label
any given line with its equation (x − y = 3). Dash construction lines. Getting tangency
visually right matters more than a perfectly accurate curve.

Circuits — resistor as a zig-zag or open rectangle, cell as long/short parallel lines,
capacitor as two parallel plates, inductor as loops, meters as circles labelled A or V.
Label components with their values and mark current direction. Series vs parallel needs
to be visibly right.

Graphs — both axes with arrowheads and the printed labels/units, origin marked, plus any
intercept, asymptote or turning point the answer hinges on. The curve's shape should be
unmistakable (linear vs parabolic vs exponential vs saturating vs sinusoidal).

Ray optics — dashed principal axis, lens as a double-arrow ellipse or mirror as an arc
with hatching behind it, F and 2F marked, object as an upright arrow, a couple of
construction rays with arrowheads, image on the correct side and orientation.

Mechanics / free-body — blocks as rectangles, pulleys as circles, strings as lines,
inclines with the angle arc labelled (θ = 30°), each force a labelled arrow (mg, N, T, f),
given masses and lengths marked.

Geometry / vectors — vertices labelled, angles arced and labelled, equal sides ticked,
construction lines dashed, vectors arrowheaded and named.

Molecular structures — skeletal line-angle formulae: vertices are carbons, heteroatoms
written as <text> (O, N, Cl, OH, NH₂, COOH). Double bond as two parallel lines, triple as
three. Benzene as a hexagon with three alternating inner double bonds. For stereochemistry,
a bold wedge as a filled narrow-to-wide polygon and a dashed bond via stroke-dasharray,
with R/S or cis/trans labelled when the question turns on it. These are the ones I'd most
expect to need fixing by hand, so if a structure is too intricate to be faithful, flagging
it is better than approximating it.

Apparatus (calorimeter, U-tube, manometer, Young's double slit, potentiometer, Wheatstone
bridge) — outline the setup, light fill or hatching for liquids, label every named part and
dimension.
